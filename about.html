Ben Colella

I picked up a camera during Covid, mostly out of boredom — my parents had an old one gathering dust, and locked-down Milwaukee turned out to be a decent place to learn how to use it. I shot everything: streets, architecture, cityscapes, the geometry of a city that wasn't going anywhere. Somewhere in that process I found what actually held my attention, which was the built environment and the way people move through it. That interest never really left.

It deepened when I started traveling. I'm drawn to places most people skip — the secondary cities, the overlooked regions, the stretches of the world that exist entirely on their own terms without caring whether anyone is watching. In those places, the camera becomes a tool for reading a place carefully: how it's organized, how it breathes, what it looks like when life just happens inside it.

My background is in real estate and urban planning, and that lens follows me everywhere I go. I notice infrastructure the way other people notice food. I'm fascinated by the chaos of a dense urban block as much as the silence of an open landscape.

The work spans landscapes, cities, architecture, and street life across dozens of countries — but it's less a travel archive than an ongoing study of the human environment in all its forms. The dramatic and the mundane. The ancient and the half-finished. The places that make you stop, and the ones that reward you for not stopping.
